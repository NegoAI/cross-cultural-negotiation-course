## Question 1 — Interests & BATNAs (26/30)

This is your strongest section. Both sides' interests are correctly identified, genuinely ranked, and explained with evidence from the case rather than simply asserted. Your observation that Nora's true interest "is not simply forming a JV but actually gaining the technical expertise to compete in the future" is exactly the right move: you looked behind the stated position to the underlying interest. Both BATNA assessments also point in the right strategic direction — Nora's alternative is weak, Sakari's is stronger.

Three things kept this answer from the top band:

- **Nora's BATNA is in the case, by name.** Mr. Hashim explicitly considers Cisco, Samsung, and AT&T as potential partners. Your answer — "find another technology partner" — is the generic version of an answer the case makes specific. Naming the alternatives and assessing their quality would have turned a correct answer into a strong one.
- **Sakari's BATNA is framed too narrowly, and one conclusion is overstated.** You present Sakari's choice as a binary — Nora or the UK — and conclude that walking away "would mean they lose access to the Asian market." That is not correct: walking away loses *Malaysia and the TMB contract*, not Asia. Sakari could still pursue its Asian-growth interest through another market — Indonesia or Thailand, which the case itself names as the JV's next targets — or with a different local partner elsewhere. These options are less feasible in the short term, but a BATNA analysis must map the full set of alternatives; collapsing it to one option makes Sakari look weaker at the negotiation table than it really is.
- **The analysis stops just short of its payoff.** With Nora facing a TMB deadline it cannot miss, and Sakari's alternatives real but slower, both parties have strong reasons to close — which is precisely the argument for why a zone of agreement exists. You had all the pieces; the final synthesis of the power balance is implied but never stated. One smaller omission: Sakari's documented concern about the efficiency and quality of the Malaysian workforce — one of the main issues of the January meeting — does not appear in its interest set.

## Question 2 — Mr. Hashim's email to Mr. Kuusisto (24/30)

Your communication-context call is correct and correctly grounded in Hall's framework: Finland is a low-context culture, so the email should be direct, explicit, and structured — and yours is. The email is disciplined and covers all five outstanding issues. Two of your proposed solutions show real integrative thinking: separating control from equity (Nora keeps majority ownership while Sakari gains a stronger voice in technological decisions) and a phased knowledge-transfer process that protects Sakari's intellectual property.

What is missing:

- **The question asked for steps to reconcile, and reconciliation is the layer your email skips.** The February meeting ended with Nora asking Sakari's senior accountant to leave the room. An email written "to reconcile" needs to acknowledge that rupture and propose a relationship-repair step — a visit to Helsinki, a gesture to restart on better terms, a joint technical workshop. Your email moves straight to the issue positions and leaves the hardest part of the task unaddressed.
- **Two of your five solutions are placeholders rather than proposals.** "A balanced solution" on the financial issues names no mechanism — for example a mid-point or sales-tiered royalty, or a defined split of the expatriate salary subsidy. "A neutral solution" on arbitration names no venue — proposing a specific neutral seat (Singapore, for instance) is the classic resolution of exactly this impasse.
- **The sentence-by-sentence explanations are labels, not reasoning.** "Direct and focuses on task" or "structured list separating problems clearly" describe the sentence; the exam asked *why each sentence fits a Finnish reader*. Only one of your explanations (assertive communication) attempts a cultural justification. For instance: why open without small talk? Why does a numbered structure suit a low-context counterpart? Why is written communication itself well suited to Finnish reserve?

## Question 3 — Cultural dimensions analysis (24/30)

You selected five legitimate dimensions, mapped each in the right direction, anchored each to at least one fact from the case, and closed each with a constructive recommendation — that last habit goes beyond what the question asked and is to your credit. The universalism–particularism reading of the arbitration clash is apt.

Three issues held the score down:

- **The selection misses the case's most powerful dimension: Power Distance.** Malaysia is among the highest power-distance cultures in the world and Finland among the most egalitarian, and the case is saturated with it: the vice-chairman-led delegation, the offence caused by Mr. Pekkarinen's arrogance, the outrage at the expatriate pay differentials. Your first two choices — high/low context and affective/neutral — overlap substantially, resting on the same evidence of Finnish reserve; that overlap spent a slot Power Distance should have taken. The question asked for the *most relevant* five, so the quality of the selection is itself part of the answer.
- **No theorist is named anywhere in this section.** Trompenaars and Hofstede never appear, although the exam explicitly asked you to reference the theories and models studied in class. Attribution is not decoration; it shows you know where each analytical tool comes from and what it can and cannot do.
- **The uncertainty-avoidance entry rests on the weakest evidence.** Sakari's protection of its intellectual property is primarily a control-and-trust issue, not an uncertainty-avoidance one. The dimension label is right; the mechanism attached to it is not.

Your tool choice is sound — Aligning Work Practices is genuine course material — and your justification follows the right logic: the conflicts are cultural, rooted in different ways of working, so the answer is one shared work culture rather than two colliding ones. Your five-step roadmap is sensible, and steps 2 and 3 instinctively reproduce two other tools from the course — Common Purpose and Team Ground Rules — without naming them, which is both to your credit and a missed opportunity.

What a top answer would have added:

- **The tool's actual mechanics.** Your answer names Aligning Work Practices and states its goal, but never shows what the tool has the team *do*: surface each side's existing practices across meetings, decision-making, communication and feedback; compare them; negotiate the common practice explicitly. Without the mechanics, the tool remains a name rather than an instrument.
- **An explicit mapping between the tool and the roadmap** — which step is where the tool does its work, and how.

- **Applying the course to your own script.** Addressing a mixed Malaysian–Finnish room is itself a cross-cultural communication task. A strong answer designs the delivery — explicit structure for the Finnish side, relational warmth for the Malaysian side — rather than a culture-neutral speech.
